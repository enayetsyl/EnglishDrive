Words whose sole purpose is to demonstrate a rule. **Exempt from held-vocabulary restrictions.** Taught explicitly before any graded use; may appear in CW, HW, PT including as graded answers; **never** dictation or spelling items; not added to File 2; not counted as fresh or held vocabulary.

| Exemplar | Demonstrates | Introduced |
|---|---|---|
| water | concrete uncountable — no plural, takes *how much* | Mon |
| honey | concrete uncountable | Mon |
| money | concrete uncountable | Mon |
| rice | concrete uncountable | Mon |
| story | plural rule: consonant + y → -ies | Sun |
| box | plural rule: word ending in x → -es | Sun |
| baby | plural rule: consonant + y → -ies | Sun |
| day | plural rule: vowel + y → just -s | Sun |
| key | plural rule: vowel + y → just -s | Sun |

**9 exemplars.** The four mass nouns exist because the held pool carries only five abstract uncountables (*patience, mercy, gratitude, science, honesty*) and none concrete — sorting *patience* is a harder first move than sorting *water*. The five plural-rule words exist because the held W1–W4 set has no -x noun, no consonant+y noun clean enough to demonstrate -ies, and no vowel+y noun at all. All nine are gradeable but never dictation items.

**No staging spine.** Unlike an article block's fixed exception set, countability has no progressive exception list. The week is organised around the two questions — *Can I count this?* and *How much or How many?* — not around introducing exemplars day by day. The plural-rule words are used Sunday; the mass nouns from Monday onward.

# Class 1 (Sunday) — Plurals: making more than one

**Objective.** Students form regular plurals (-s, -es, y-words both ways) and the held irregulars confidently, and end the lesson understanding that some nouns have no plural at all — the doorway to Monday's countability work.

**Exemplars today:** `story · box · baby · day · key` (plural-rule demonstrators)

| Step | Time | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0–5 | What a plural is — recall |
| 2 | 5–17 | The regular rules (-s · -es · y-words both ways) |
| 3 | 17–24 | The irregulars you must learn |
| 4 | 24–30 | Diagnostic round |
| 5 | 30–33 | The bridge — nouns with no plural |
| 6 | 33 | CW-1 distribution |
| 7 | 33–35 | Exit Check |

**Note on pace.** Sunday has room. If classwork does not finish in class it goes home with HW-1 (§9.3) — do not speed through the rules to complete CW-1 in the room. The one part that must not be cut is Step 5, the bridge into Monday.

### Step 1 — What a plural is (0–5 min)

**Books closed.** Write on the board:

```
one flower  →  two flowers
one bus     →  two ______
```

> "You have known this since Class 1. **One flower, two flowers.** When we mean more than one, the noun changes. We call the 'more than one' form the **plural**."

> "Fill in the second line for me — one bus, two…?"

Someone will say *buses*. Someone may say *buss*. Do not correct yet — write both up.

> "Two answers. By the end of today you will know exactly which is right, and why. This week is about number and quantity — how English shows *how many* and *how much* of a thing. It starts here, with plurals."

**Teacher note.** Do not rush this opener even though the class knows plurals. The whole week's countability idea is built on one question — *does this noun even have a plural?* — so the plural form must be secure and conscious, not automatic, before Monday.

### Step 2 — The regular rules (5–17 min)

Teach one rule at a time. Say each example aloud, then write it. There is no hurry here.

**(a) The ordinary rule — add -s (5–8).**

```
flower → flowers    clock → clocks    train → trains    poem → poems    tiger → tigers
```

> "Most nouns just add **-s**. Say them with me." Class reads the plurals chorally.

**(b) When a word ends in s, ss, sh, ch, x — add -es (8–11).**

Write the singulars only: `bus · glass · beach · box`

> "Try to add just **-s**. Say *buss*. Say *glasss*." Let them hear it fail.

> "You cannot hear the ending. When a word ends in **s, ss, sh, ch, x**, we add **-es** so you can hear it."

```
bus → buses    glass → glasses    beach → beaches    box → boxes
```

> "Say each one. Hear the extra sound at the end — *bus-es*, *box-es*."

**Teacher note.** *box* is an exemplar — the held W1–W4 set has no word ending in x to demonstrate this rule. Teach it here; it may be graded from CW-1. Not a vocabulary or dictation word.

**(c) Words ending in y (11–17).**

Write two groups, one above the other:

```
story · baby · country · library
day · key
```

> "All of these end in **y**. But look at the letter **just before** the y — that decides what happens."

**Consonant + y → -ies.** Point at the first group.

> "*Story, baby, country, library* — a consonant comes before the y: *r, b, r, r*. The y changes to **i**, and we add **-es**."

```
story → stories    baby → babies    country → countries    library → libraries
```

> "Say them. *Sto-ries. Ba-bies.* The y is gone; **-ies** takes its place."

**Vowel + y → just add -s.** Point at the second group.

> "But *day* and *key* — a vowel comes before the y: *a*, *e*. When a vowel is before the y, the y does **not** change. Just add **-s**."

```
day → days    key → keys
```

> "So the y-words split in two. Consonant before y — *-ies*. Vowel before y — just *-s*. Look before the y, then decide."

**Teacher note.** *story* and *baby* are exemplars for the -ies rule; *day* and *key* are exemplars for the vowel+y rule (the held set has no clean example of either). *country* and *library* are held. Teach all six the same way; the exemplars carry no special label for the student.

### Step 3 — The irregulars you must learn (17–24 min)

> "Those rules cover most nouns. But English has a few words that follow **no rule**. You cannot work them out — you must know them. Here are the ones we use this year."

Build the table, class saying each plural aloud twice:

| Singular | Plural | |
|---|---|---|
| child | **children** | not *childs* |
| deer | **deer** | no change at all |
| hero | **heroes** | |

> "*Child* becomes **children** — a brand-new word. *Deer* does not change — one deer, two deer. *Hero* takes **-es**."

> "How do you learn these? You do not work them out. You remember them, like a name."

**Teacher note.** Keep this set to the three held irregulars only. Do not add *man/men*, *foot/feet*, *mouse/mice* — they are not in the held vocabulary and widen the load past the §2 re-walk. If a student offers one, accept it as correct and say "yes — another one to remember," but do not put it on the board to be learned.

### Step 4 — Diagnostic round (24–30 min)

**Books closed. This tells you who needs help — note it, do not stop to re-teach spelling.**

Call each noun by roll; the student gives the plural aloud. Whole class repeats the correct form.

| Teacher says | Correct plural | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| bus | buses | -es (ends in s) |
| child | children | irregular |
| country | countries | consonant + y |
| deer | deer | irregular |
| glass | glasses | -es (ends in ss) |
| beach | beaches | -es (ends in ch) |
| hero | heroes | irregular |
| day | days | vowel + y |
| museum | museums | -s |
| box | boxes | -es (ends in x) |
| library | libraries | consonant + y |
| key | keys | vowel + y |

**Teacher note.** Mark privately who missed *children*, the -es words, and the -ies words. You will pair-support them during Monday's sort — a student who cannot form *children* cannot use the plural test on *child*. Do **not** spend the remaining time re-teaching rules; the diagnostic is a probe, not a remediation slot.

### Step 5 — The bridge: nouns with no plural (30–33 min)

**The most important three minutes of the day. Do not skip, and do not let an overrunning Step 2 eat it.**

Write one word: `water`

> "Give me the plural of *water*."

Let the silence sit. Someone may try *waters*.

> "Say *waters* aloud. Does it sound right? Have you ever asked for *two waters*?"

> "You cannot. **Water has no plural.** You cannot count it in ones — one water, two waters — it does not work."

Write two more: `honey · money`

> "Try these. Two honeys? Two moneys? No. Some nouns simply have no plural, because you cannot count them in ones."

> "That is tomorrow's whole lesson. Some nouns you **can** count — flower, bus, child. Some you **cannot** — water, honey, money. Which kind a noun is decides everything about how we use it. Tomorrow we find out why it matters."

**Teacher note.** Do not name "countable / uncountable" today — Monday's discovery depends on the class meeting the split fresh. Plant only the fact: some nouns have no plural. Leave *water · honey · money* on the board if it survives to Monday.

### Step 6 — CW-1 (33 min)

Distribute CW-1. Model item 1 on the board (§9.3). Students begin; whatever does not finish goes home with HW-1.

### Step 7 — Exit Check (33–35 min)

Teacher says the noun; one student per prompt gives the plural. Eight prompts, one per student.

| # | Prompt | Answer | Rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | bus | buses | -es |
| 2 | child | children | irregular |
| 3 | country | countries | consonant + y |
| 4 | day | days | vowel + y |
| 5 | deer | deer | no change |
| 6 | glass | glasses | -es |
| 7 | hero | heroes | irregular |
| 8 | baby | babies | consonant + y |

# Class 2 (Monday) — Can I count this?

**Objective.** Students sort nouns into countable and uncountable, using the plural test to decide, understand that uncountable nouns never take a plural, and meet *some* and *many* as a first pair of quantity words.

**Exemplars today:** `water · honey · money · rice` (concrete uncountables)

| Step | Time | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0–4 | Recall the bridge |
| 2 | 4–12 | Discover the two groups |
| 3 | 12–20 | Uncountables that trip you up |
| 4 | 20–27 | Guided sort |
| 5 | 27–30 | *some* and *many* |
| 6 | 30–32 | Clue Card + CW-2 |
| 7 | 32–35 | Exit Check |

### Step 1 — Recall the bridge (0–4 min)

**Books closed.** Write: `water · honey · money`

> "Yesterday you told me these three have something in common. What?" → *No plural.*

> "Right. You cannot count them in ones. Today we give the two kinds of noun their names, and we find out why the difference matters."

Write and leave up all lesson:

```
① Can I count this?
```

### Step 2 — Discover the two groups (4–12 min)

Write four nouns in a single row — **do not sort them yet:**

```
flower     water     clock     honey
```

> "I am going to count these. One flower, two flowers — easy. One clock, two clocks — easy." Write the plurals above *flower* and *clock*.

> "Now — one water, two… waters?" Pause. "One honey, two honeys?" Let the wrongness land.

> "So these four split into two kinds. Sort them for me — which can I count, which can I not?"

Take answers; move the words into two columns on the board:

| I can count these | I cannot count these |
|---|---|
| flower, clock | water, honey |

> "The ones you can count in ones, we call **countable**. The ones you cannot, we call **uncountable**." Write the two headings.

> "And your **test** is the one from yesterday — **does it have a plural?** If yes, it is countable. If no plural, it is uncountable."

**Teacher note.** *water* and *honey* are exemplars; *money* and *rice* join in Step 3. The sort is discovered, not announced — resist naming the columns until the class has physically split the four words.

### Step 3 — Uncountables that trip you up (12–20 min)

**(a) More concrete uncountables (12–14).** Add to the uncountable column: `money · rice`

> "Money — can you count it in ones? You count *taka*, but not *moneys*. Rice — you count *grains* or *bowls*, but never *rices*. Both uncountable."

**(b) Abstract uncountables (14–17).** Write: `patience · mercy · gratitude · science · honesty`

> "These are harder — you cannot even see them. But the test still works. Two patiences? Two mercies? No plural. So — uncountable, every one."

Write the wrong forms and cross them out with the class watching:

```
patiences (X)    mercies (X)    moneys (X)    rices (X)
```

> "These forms do not exist. If your pen ever writes one, stop — the noun is uncountable."

**(c) The measuring trap (17–20).** Write: `hour · minute · quarter`

> "Careful with these. They measure something — but can you count them? One hour, two hours. One minute, two minutes. **Countable.** A word can be about time and still be countable, as long as you can count it in ones."

**Teacher note.** Do not introduce *time* itself (which is uncountable) as a contrast — it is not a held word and the added abstraction is Class 6 territory. Keep the trap concrete: *hour/minute/quarter* are countable because they take plurals. That is enough.

### Step 4 — Guided sort (20–27 min)

Read each aloud; class answers **C** or **U**; ask "how do you know?" every time. The answer is always the plural test.

| Noun | Answer | Test |
|---|---|---|
| flower | C | *flowers* — has a plural |
| water | U | no plural |
| child | C | *children* — has a plural |
| mercy | U | no plural |
| bus | C | *buses* — has a plural |
| honey | U | no plural |
| hour | C | *hours* — has a plural |
| money | U | no plural |
| poem | C | *poems* — has a plural |
| rice | U | no plural |

> "Every single time, the same test decides it. Can I make it plural? Yes — countable. No — uncountable."

**Attribution watch.** When *water* or *rice* comes up in any sentence, phrase it *Allah gives us water from the rain* — never *the river gives us water*. Watch this all week.

### Step 5 — *some* and *many* (27–30 min)

> "Now that you know the two kinds, here is the first pair of quantity words. **Many** goes with things you can count. **Some** goes with both."

```
many flowers    many buses    many children     (countable → many)
some water      some flowers                     (some → both)
```

> "*Many* needs countable nouns — many flowers, never *many water*. *Some* is friendly — it goes with either."

Quick oral round: "*___ rice* — some or many?" (some) · "*___ prizes*?" (many or some) · "*___ honey*?" (some).

**Teacher note.** Keep this to three minutes. *some* and *many* are not on Thursday's test — they are everyday words that reinforce the sort. Do not drill to mastery; Tuesday adds *much / a few / a little* and Wednesday revises. Today they are just the first evidence that countability decides word choice.

### Step 6 — Clue Card + CW-2 (30–32 min)

Build rows 1–3 of the Countability Chart with the class (count in ones · plural test · *a/an*). Leave rows 4–5 blank — those are Tuesday's. Distribute CW-2; model item 1. Unfinished CW goes home with HW-2.

### Step 7 — Exit Check (32–35 min)

Teacher says the noun; one student per prompt gives **C** or **U** and the test. Eight prompts.

| # | Prompt | Answer | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | clock | C | *clocks* — has a plural |
| 2 | water | U | no plural |
| 3 | child | C | *children* — has a plural |
| 4 | patience | U | no plural |
| 5 | bus | C | *buses* — has a plural |
| 6 | honey | U | no plural |
| 7 | hour | C | *hours* — has a plural |
| 8 | rice | U | no plural |

Spread: C ×4 · U ×4, no run beyond 2.

# Class 3 (Tuesday) — much, a few, a little → How much / How many

**Objective.** Students choose *much / many / some / a few / a little* by countability, then use that same decision to build the exam's *How much / How many … is there / are there* table correctly. **Heaviest day — protect the table.**

**Rehearsal set (ungraded, oral):** `garden · patience · bee · forest · clock`

| Step | Time | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0–3 | Recall the sort |
| 2 | 3–15 | The quantifiers (hard cap 12 min) |
| 3 | 15–18 | The link — many→How many, much→How much |
| 4 | 18–20 | Chart rows 4–5 + is/are there |
| 5 | 20–30 | The Q8 table — guided build |
| 6 | 30–32 | CW-3 distribution |
| 7 | 32–35 | Exit Check |

### Step 1 — Recall the sort (0–3 min)

**Books closed.** Rapid round, class answers C or U:

`flower · water · child · money · bus · rice · hour · honey`

> "One test decides all of them. What is it?" → *Does it have a plural?*

> "Hold that. Today countability stops being just a label — it starts **deciding** which words you use."

### Step 2 — The quantifiers (3–15 min · HARD CAP)

> "Yesterday you met *some* and *many*. Here are three more quantity words. Each one cares whether the noun is countable."

**(a) much — the partner of many (3–7).**

```
many prizes    (countable)          much rice     (uncountable)
many buses     (countable)          much water    (uncountable)
```

> "**Many** for things you count. **Much** for things you cannot. *Many prizes* — but *much rice*, never *many rice*."

**(b) a few / a little (7–12).**

```
a few flowers    (countable)         a little honey    (uncountable)
a few children   (countable)         a little water    (uncountable)
```

> "**A few** for countable — a few flowers. **A little** for uncountable — a little honey. Same split, again. The noun decides."

Two quick oral rounds only:

> "*___ rice* — a few or a little?" (a little) · "*___ prizes*?" (a few) · "*___ water*?" (a little) · "*___ buses*?" (a few)

**Teacher note.** **Stop at 12 minutes even if unfinished.** These four words are *not* on Thursday's test — they are taught for everyday speech under the §2 cap. Their only job today is to show, one more time, that countability governs word choice, and to set up the question words. Do not drill to mastery. The table in Step 5 is what carries marks; protect its time.

### Step 3 — The link (15–18 min)

> "Now watch what happens when we turn these into questions."

Write, aligned:

```
many flowers   →   How many flowers?
much water     →   How much water?
```

> "**Many** becomes **How many.** **Much** becomes **How much.** It is the same choice you already know — just asked as a question. If the noun is countable, **How many.** If uncountable, **How much.**"

### Step 4 — Chart rows 4–5 (18–20 min)

Complete the Countability Chart with the class:

| | Countable | Uncountable |
|---|---|---|
| **Which question word?** | **How many** | **How much** |
| **Which verb in the table?** | **are there** | **is there** |

> "One more piece. Countable nouns are plural — so they take **are there**. Uncountable nouns take **is there**. *How many flowers **are there**? How much water **is there**?*"

### Step 5 — The Q8 table (20–30 min)

**This is the exam. Give it the time.**

Draw the three-column table exactly as the paper prints it:

| How much / How many | oranges · water · flowers · honey | is there? / are there? |
|---|---|---|

> "This is your exam question, word for word: *Make proper sentences from the table.* You pick the right question word, the right noun, and the right ending."

Build two aloud with the class:

> "*Oranges* — countable. So — **How many oranges are there?** *Water* — uncountable. So — **How much water is there?**"

Now drill the **double choice** — where marks are lost:

| Teacher says | Correct sentence |
|---|---|
| flowers | How many flowers are there? |
| honey | How much honey is there? |
| oranges | How many oranges are there? |
| water | How much water is there? |

> "Every sentence, two decisions. Pick *How much* and then write *are there* — you lose the mark. The question word and the verb must **agree**: How many … are there. How much … is there."

**Teacher note.** The single most common error is a right question word with the wrong verb — *How much water are there.* Correct it every time by asking "countable or uncountable?" then "so which verb?" Both halves must match. This is the Analyze step of the block.

### Step 6 — CW-3 distribution (30–32 min)

Distribute CW-3 (the table build); model item 1 on the board. Unfinished CW goes home with HW-3.

**Teacher note.** The self-try is introduced on Wednesday, not today — Tuesday's time belongs to the table. Do not model the self-try here.

### Step 7 — Exit Check (32–35 min)

Teacher says the noun; the student completes the question aloud with the correct verb. Eight prompts.

| # | Prompt | Answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | oranges | How many oranges are there? |
| 2 | water | How much water is there? |
| 3 | prizes | How many prizes are there? |
| 4 | honey | How much honey is there? |
| 5 | flowers | How many flowers are there? |
| 6 | rice | How much rice is there? |
| 7 | books | How many books are there? |
| 8 | money | How much money is there? |

# Class 4 (Wednesday) — Revision, self-try, and test preparation

**Objective.** Students consolidate the sort and the table across mixed items, rehearse the self-try format that is graded on Thursday, and prepare for the PT. The lightest day by design — Tuesday carried the new load.

| Step | Time | Focus |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0–8 | The Q8 table — repair and secure |
| 2 | 8–16 | Mixed revision |
| 3 | 16–20 | Self-try — model and set up |
| 4 | 20–28 | Self-try — students write |
| 5 | 28–32 | Read-aloud + feedback |
| 6 | 32–33 | HW-4 distribution |

### Step 1 — The Q8 table, secured (0–8 min)

**Open here, not with the sort — this repairs any Tuesday shortfall.**

Clue Card visible. Draw a fresh three-column table:

| How much / How many | prizes · rice · flowers | is there? / are there? |

Build all three with the class, insisting on both decisions each time:

> "*Prizes* — countable — **How many prizes are there?** *Rice* — uncountable — **How much rice is there?** *Flowers* — countable — **How many flowers are there?**"

> "Do not move on until every one of you can pick the question word **and** the verb. This is Thursday's five marks."

**Teacher note.** If Tuesday ran short and the class is shaky on the double choice, spend up to 12 minutes here and trim Step 2. The table is the graded core; mixed revision is support.

### Step 2 — Mixed revision (8–16 min)

Distribute CW-4. Three quick blocks, oral first, then students write their answers on the sheet.

**(a) Sort (8–11).** C or U: `poem · money · deer · gratitude · hour · rice`

**(b) Plural (11–13).** Give the plural: `bus · child · country · glass`

**(c) Quantity (13–16).** Choose the word: "*___ water* — much or many?" (much) · "*a few* or *a little* honey?" (a little) · "*___ prizes* — much or many?" (many)

> "Three things, one idea underneath all of them: can I count this noun? Everything follows from that."

### Step 3 — Self-try: model and set up (16–20 min)

> "On Thursday you write this for marks. Today we practise it."

Model one fully on the board, saying each line:

> **Quantity word:** how much · **Noun:** rice · **Countable or uncountable?** uncountable
> **Sentence:** *How much rice is there in the bag?*

> "Three parts. Choose a quantity word. Use it in a real sentence. Then name your noun countable or uncountable — and be right, because that is a mark on its own."

### Step 4 — Self-try: students write (20–28 min)

Each student writes three sentences, each using a different quantity word (*how many · how much · some · a little · a few*), and labels the noun in each. Teacher circulates, correcting the **label line** especially — a good sentence with a wrong C/U label still loses that mark.

### Step 5 — Read-aloud + feedback (28–32 min)

Three or four students read one sentence and its label. Give quick feedback on the reasoning, not just the sentence:

> "Good — *some water*, and you called water uncountable. Correct, because…?" → *no plural.*

Distribute HW-4 (the task line is on the sheet). No modelling needed — it mirrors today's work.

### PT teacher instructions

**Part A dictation** — say each word twice, then read the full list once more at the end. All from the W4 held batch; **no exemplars** (§5.7):

`cyclone · museum · announcement · deer · hero · quarter · protect · prepare · pleasant · carefully`

**Timing guide** — A 6 min · B 9 min · C 5 min · D 4 min · E 7 min · 4 min checking. Part E is 4 marks of 34; the time reflects that production is slower per mark than recognition, not that it carries more weight.

**Part E — assign the quantity word, do not let the student choose.** On Wednesday the class *chose* their quantifier and rehearsed a sentence; if the PT let them choose again they would reproduce that rehearsed sentence and the four marks would test nothing. So the teacher **assigns** each student one quantity word by roll, and the student must compose a fresh sentence around exactly that word:

Roll 1 → **a little** · Roll 2 → **many** · Roll 3 → **a few** · Roll 4 → **much** · Roll 5 → **some** · Roll 6 → **a little** · Roll 7 → **many** · Roll 8 → **a few**

The assigned set is `much · many · a little · a few · some` — the statement quantifiers, deliberately **not** *how much / how many* (those are already graded in Parts B and C, and they force the question-table shape the student just drilled). Assigning a statement quantifier forces a different construction. If a student writes a sentence for a different word than the one assigned, line (a) is wrong and the sentence cannot earn content-accuracy.

**Two rules that void the sentence.** The self-try sentence must be (i) a **statement**, not a question — any sentence beginning with a question word or ending in a question mark scores 0 for content accuracy and organisation, because the task is to *use* the quantifier in speech, not to rebuild the Part B/C question table; and (ii) **original** — a sentence that reproduces any item from Parts B, C, or the worksheets earns no content-accuracy mark, since a copied sentence demonstrates nothing the student constructed. Both rules exist because *how much / how many* are graded in Parts B and C; without them a student could copy an interrogative from there and bypass the construction task.

**Marking the self-try** — the four criteria each score 1 or 0 (see rubric below). The assigned word fixes the required noun type, so line (d) has one correct answer: *a little / much* → uncountable · *a few / many* → countable · *some* → either (accept the label that matches the student's own noun). A correct sentence with a wrong label in line (d) still loses that mark.

# Self-construction rubric (PT Part E)

**4 marks** — Charter v1.3 §I.3, four criteria at 1 mark each, uniform across all five classes.

| Criterion | Marks | What earns it |
|---|---|---|
| Content accuracy | 1 | assigned quantity word used correctly with a noun of the right type, in an **original statement** (not a question, not copied from elsewhere on the paper) |
| Reasoning | 1 | line (d) labels the noun's countability correctly |
| Organisation | 1 | complete, readable **statement** — not an interrogative rebuilt from Parts B/C |
| Correct word-class placement | 1 | quantity word sits before the noun it modifies |
| **Total** | **4** | |

Each criterion scores **1 or 0** — no partial credit at this total. Line **(d)** carries 1 of 4: a wrong or blank countability label caps the student at 3. A question-form or copied sentence forfeits both content accuracy and organisation (see the two voiding rules in the PT teacher instructions).

**Islamic/value alignment is not scored** (Charter §I.3, Run Book §13.1). Charter §H binds every item, prompt and passage in this block in full — but a student's own sentence is marked on the four criteria above. A religiously neutral, on-topic sentence earns full marks. A sentence that conflicts with §H is flagged and corrected as a values matter, never marked down as a language error.
